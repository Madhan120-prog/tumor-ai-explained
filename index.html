Tumour AI, explained

How AI learns to outline a lung tumour on CT

Starting from a single neuron: how a neural network is built, how its shape had to change for pictures, 3D scans and outlines, the models we compare for outlining tumours, how we train and test them fairly, and finally a lung CT scan flowing through the working system. Every chapter has a Simple and a Detailed view.

    Part 1 · A general neural network
    Chapter 1

    A neuron is a weighted vote

    A neuron takes a few numbers in. It multiplies each by a weight (how much it trusts that input), adds them up together with a bias (its starting lean), and passes the total through an activation that decides how strongly it fires.

    Picture a single detective weighing two clues. Each clue gets a level of trust (a weight), and the detective starts with a hunch (the bias). Add it up: if the total is high enough, they say “suspect”.

    The weights and the bias are the knobs. Training a network means finding good knob settings. Every model later on this page, including our 3D U-Net with 5.6 million knobs, is this one idea repeated.

    Written out: y = f(w₁·x₁ + w₂·x₂ + b). Here f is a sigmoid, 1 / (1 + e−z), which squeezes any total into 0 to 1. Real networks use other activations too; our U-Net uses LeakyReLU, which keeps positive totals and shrinks negative ones.

    Chapter 2

    AND, OR, and the XOR wall

    Give the neuron inputs that are only 0 or 1, and make the activation a simple step: fire (1) if the total is above 0, otherwise stay quiet (0). Now one neuron can act as a logic gate. Move the knobs, or load a preset, and watch the truth table and the dividing line.

    Filled dot = neuron says 1, hollow = 0. The shaded side of the line is where it fires.

    AND fires only when both inputs are 1. OR fires when at least one is. XOR means “one or the other, but not both”: (0,1) and (1,0) give 1, while (0,0) and (1,1) give 0. Try it: no setting of the three knobs works.

    ProblemA single neuron always splits the square with one straight line. XOR's two 1-points sit on opposite corners, so no straight line can put them on one side and the two 0-points on the other.
    FixUse more than one neuron, arranged in layers (next chapter).
    One detective with one rule of thumb can say “both clues point to them” or “either clue does”. They cannot say “exactly one clue does, but not both”.

    Why the presets work. AND: w₁ = 1, w₂ = 1, b = −1.5. Only 1 + 1 − 1.5 = 0.5 is above zero, so only (1,1) fires. OR: same weights, b = −0.5. Any single 1 gives 0.5, so three points fire.

    Why it is always a line. The neuron fires where w₁·x₁ + w₂·x₂ + b > 0. The border, w₁·x₁ + w₂·x₂ + b = 0, is the equation of a straight line. The weights tilt it and the bias slides it. Problems a line can solve are called linearly separable. XOR is the classic one that is not; pointing this out (Minsky and Papert, 1969) slowed neural-network research for years.

    Chapter 3

    Layers: a team beats one detective

    Put two neurons side by side in a hidden layer, and a third neuron after them. Each hidden neuron draws its own line: neuron A is an OR, neuron B is a NAND (“not both”). The output neuron is an AND of the two: fire when A and B fire. That is exactly XOR.

    Watch what the hidden layer does: it moves the points. In the hidden layer's view, (0,1) and (1,0) land on the same spot, and now a single straight line separates the 1s from the 0s. Each layer turns the clues into new, more useful clues, until the last neuron's job is easy. That is the whole idea behind “deep” learning: stack layers.

    Two junior detectives each check one thing (“is at least one clue there?”, “are not both there?”). The senior detective only has to read their two reports and agree with both.

    The weights. A = step(x₁ + x₂ − 0.5) is OR. B = step(−x₁ − x₂ + 1.5) is NAND. Output = step(A + B − 1.5) is AND. Nine knobs in total: 3 neurons × (2 weights + 1 bias).

    Why the activation matters. Without a bend in the middle (the step here, or tanh or LeakyReLU later), two layers of weighted sums are still just one weighted sum, and you are back to a single straight line. The activation is what lets layers build something new.

    Chapter 4

    Learning: the network finds its own knobs

    Above, we set the knobs by hand. Our tumour model has 5.6 million of them, far too many for that. So networks learn their knobs with a loop that repeats thousands of times:

    1. Guess. Run the examples through the network.
    2. How wrong? Compare with the answers and boil it down to one number, the loss.
    3. Who caused it? Work backwards to find how much each knob added to the error (backpropagation).
    4. Nudge. Turn every knob a little in the direction that lowers the loss.

    Orange = the network says 1, grey = it says 0. The corner dots are the four XOR examples.

    This network has 2 inputs, 4 hidden neurons and 1 output: 17 knobs, all starting random. Nobody tells it “use an OR and a NAND”. Press Train and watch the loss fall and the orange area bend around the corners. lr (the learning rate) is the size of each nudge: try 2.5 for faster but wobblier, 0.05 for steady but slow. Now and then a random start gets stuck; new random knobs usually fix it.

    After every case with a known answer, the chief says how far off the team was. The blame is passed back down the ranks, and each detective adjusts how much they trust each clue.

    The loss here is binary cross-entropy: saying 0.9 when the answer is 1 costs a little, saying 0.1 costs a lot. Our tumour model uses Dice plus cross-entropy (chapter 15).

    Backpropagation is the chain rule from calculus, applied layer by layer from the output back to the input. It gives, for each knob, the slope of the loss: which way to turn it, and how much it matters. Then gradient descent moves every knob a step of size lr down that slope.

    The same network in PyTorch, the library our models use:

    net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(2, 4), nn.Tanh(),     # hidden layer: 4 × 3 = 12 knobs
                    nn.Linear(4, 1), nn.Sigmoid())  # output: 5 knobs
opt = torch.optim.SGD(net.parameters(), lr=0.5)
for step in range(2000):
    loss = nn.BCELoss()(net(X), y)   # 1 guess, 2 how wrong
    opt.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()                  # 3 who caused it
    opt.step()                       # 4 nudge
    Part 2 · Changing the shape for pictures, scans and outlines
    Chapter 5

    Pictures need sliding filters

    To a computer, a picture is a grid of numbers: one per pixel, dark for small numbers and bright for big ones. Below is a tiny 10 × 10 picture of a bright spot on a dark background. Could we feed it to the network from Part 1?

    ProblemGive every pixel its own weight and a single neuron needs 262,144 knobs for a 512 × 512 picture. Worse, it learns “a spot here” and has to learn the same spot all over again for every other place it could be.
    FixA convolution: one small 3 × 3 filter (9 knobs) slides over the whole picture, doing a weighted sum at every position. The same filter finds the same pattern anywhere.
    ✱ =

    Click any cell of the output to see how it was worked out. Orange = a strong positive answer, green = a strong negative one.

    Each filter is a small pattern detector. The edge filter lights up only at the spot's border, where the brightness changes; inside the spot and out in the dark it gives zero. The blur filter averages its neighbours. The vertical-edge filter tells the left side of the spot (orange) from the right side (green).

    In a real network the 9 numbers in each filter are knobs, learned with the same guess → how wrong → blame → nudge loop from chapter 4. Nobody hand-picks them. And there are many filters per layer, each learning to spot something different.

    Instead of one detective staring at the whole city map at once, every detective carries the same small magnifying glass and checks the map square by square, looking for one tell-tale sign.

    The sum. At each position, multiply the 9 picture numbers under the filter by the 9 filter numbers and add them up (plus a bias, left out here). That one number is the output pixel. Sliding over all 8 × 8 positions where the filter fits gives an 8 × 8 output. Adding a border of zeros around the picture (padding) keeps the output 10 × 10; our U-Net uses padding so sizes stay neat.

    Channels. A layer with 32 filters makes 32 output pictures, called feature maps or channels. The next layer's filters look at all 32 at once, so they can combine simple signs (edges) into more complex ones (corners, round blobs).

    Knob count. A 3 × 3 filter has 9 weights per input channel, no matter how big the picture is. That is why a network for 512 × 512 pictures does not need billions of knobs. In 3D (chapter 9) the filter becomes a 3 × 3 × 3 cube: 27 knobs.

    Chapter 6

    Zooming out answers “is it there?”

    One 3 × 3 filter only sees a tiny patch. To see bigger things, networks repeat two steps: filter, then pool, which halves the picture by keeping the strongest value in each 2 × 2 square. Every halving means each cell now stands for twice as wide an area. After a few rounds, one cell sees the whole picture, and the network can give one answer: yes, there is a spot, or no.

    The orange squares on the picture show how much of it one cell at each step can see.

    This is how a picture classifier works: lots of filter-and-pool steps, then a final yes or no (or “cat”, “dog”, “car”). It was the big breakthrough of 2012, and it is good at saying whether something is in a picture.

    ProblemPress “Move the spot”: the answer stays exactly the same. Zooming out threw the position away. It can say “yes, there is a spot”, but not where, and an outline needs exactly that.
    FixInstead of one answer for the whole picture, ask for an answer at every pixel (next chapter).
    Reports go up the ranks. Street detectives report to district heads, district heads to the chief. The chief now knows “there is a gang in this city”, but the reports along the way dropped the street names.

    Receptive field. The area of the original picture that one cell depends on. Two 3 × 3 filters in a row see 5 × 5; each 2 × 2 pool doubles it. After four pools, cells see areas 16 times wider, which is why the deep cells in a U-Net can tell a tumour from a blood vessel by its surroundings.

    More channels as it shrinks. Networks usually double the number of filters at every halving, trading “where” for “what”. Our U-Net goes 32 → 64 → 128 → 256 channels as the picture shrinks.

    Max pooling keeps the biggest of 4 values: “was the sign anywhere in this square?”. Some networks instead use a filter that jumps 2 pixels at a time (a stride of 2), which learns its own way to shrink.

    Chapter 7

    Outlining: zoom out, then back in

    An outline needs a yes or no for every pixel: is this pixel part of the shape? Below is a 32 × 32 picture of a bright, lumpy shape. The dashed green line is its true outline. Here are the first two ways people tried to draw it.

    Green dashed = true outline. Orange = the outline the method found. The overlap score is 100% when they match exactly.

    • Pixel-by-pixel classifier (2012). Cut a small patch around every pixel and ask the yes/no network from chapter 6 about each one. The outline is decent, but it means one full network run per pixel: 1,024 runs for this tiny picture, 262,144 for one 512 × 512 scan slice. Painfully slow, and each patch sees very little of its surroundings.
    • Fully convolutional network, FCN (2015). Run the zoom-out from chapter 6 once on the whole picture (the encoder), then stretch the small result back up to full size in one go (the decoder). One run instead of a thousand. But look at the outline: the lumps are gone, because by the bottom of the zoom-out the fine detail of the edge has been thrown away.
    ProblemStretching a 4 × 4 summary back to 32 × 32 cannot bring back edges it no longer knows about. The outline comes out blurry and rounded.
    FixKeep the sharp detail from the way down and hand it back on the way up (next chapter).
    The chief knows roughly which district the gang is in. Asked to draw the exact border on a street map, all they can do is draw a fat circle around the district.

    Why FCN is fast. Filters do not care how big the picture is, so the same network that answered yes/no for a patch can run over the whole picture at once, giving a small grid of answers instead of one. Neighbouring patches share almost all their work, and FCN does that shared work only once.

    Stretching back. The real FCN learned its stretching (an up-convolution, or transposed convolution) and added a little detail from earlier layers. That helped, but its sharpest version still stretched by 8 in one step. U-Net took the idea all the way.

    The patch classifier shown is the approach of Cireşan and colleagues, who won a 2012 contest outlining nerve cells in microscope pictures this way.

    Chapter 8

    U-Net: handing the detail across

    U-Net (Ronneberger and colleagues, 2015, made for medical pictures) changes two things. It zooms back in step by step, doubling the size each time, mirroring the way down. And at every step up, it is handed a copy of the sharp picture from the same size on the way down. Those copies are the skip connections. Drawn out, the way down and the way up make a U.

    Left: the picture shrinking (what is there). Right: the answer growing back (where it is), shown in orange. Green dashed arrows: skip connections.

    The bottom of the U knows what is in the picture but has only a rough idea of where. The skip connections bring back the where: at each size, the decoder lays its rough answer over the sharp copy and tidies the edge. Turn the skip connections off and you are back to a blurry, rounded outline, just like FCN.

    The chief's rough answer is passed back down the ranks. At each rank, the detectives who worked that level on the way up hand over their own detailed notes, so by the time it reaches the street the border can be drawn house by house.
    Still to solveThis is a flat picture. A CT scan is a stack of slices, and a flat U-Net reads them one at a time.
    NextMake every filter, pool and skip three-dimensional (chapter 9).

    How the hand-over works. The decoder doubles its feature maps with an up-convolution, then concatenates (stacks) them with the encoder's feature maps of the same size, then runs two 3 × 3 filters over the stack. The filters learn how to mix “roughly here” with “sharp edge here”. In PyTorch: torch.cat([up, skip], dim=1).

    Why it took off. U-Net learned from only 30 training pictures, because the skips make outlining easy to learn, and it ran on a whole picture at once. Almost every medical outlining model since, including all three we compare in Part 3, keeps this U shape.

    What this demo simplifies. The boxes show one picture per level; a real U-Net has many feature maps per level (ours: 32, 64, 128, 256), and their values are learned, not hand-written.

    Chapter 9

    3D scans: filters become cubes

    A CT scan is not one picture. It is a stack of slices, like a loaf of sliced bread, each slice a grey picture of the body at one height. The U-Net from chapter 8 reads one slice at a time.

    ProblemOn a single slice, a ball (like a tumour) and a tube (like a blood vessel) can look exactly the same: a round bright spot. You only tell them apart by looking up and down through the slices.
    FixMake the network itself 3D. Every 3 × 3 filter becomes a 3 × 3 × 3 cube that slides through the whole stack, so the network sees shape in all three directions.

    That is the 3D U-Net (Çiçek and colleagues, 2016): the same U as chapter 8, but every filter, every halving and every skip connection works on blocks of the scan instead of flat pictures. Below is one you can turn around. Watch a block of a scan go down the U, the sharp copies cross over the top, and a 3D outline come out on the right.

    Loading the 3D view…

    Drag to turn it around. Green blocks: the way down (what is there). Orange blocks: the way up (where it is). Dashed lines: skip connections. The small orange cube on the scan is one 3 × 3 × 3 filter.

    The detectives stop reading single pages of the case file and start reading the whole file at once, so “a round shape on page 12” becomes “a ball across pages 10 to 14”, not “a pipe through every page”.

    Knobs. A 3 × 3 × 3 filter has 27 weights per input channel instead of 9, so a 3D U-Net has about three times the knobs of the same-shaped 2D one, and needs far more memory: a block of 48 × 160 × 160 voxels (3D pixels) is 1.2 million numbers per channel. That is why 3D U-Nets train on blocks cut from the scan, not the whole scan, and why ours trains on a university supercomputer (BigBlue).

    Uneven voxels. CT slices are often further apart than the pixels within a slice: in our data, slices are 3 mm apart but pixels are about 1 mm. So a voxel is a tall brick, not a cube. Our U-Net's first halving shrinks only within each slice (1 × 2 × 2), so after it the voxels are close to cubes (chapter 10).

    2D vs 3D. 3D is not always better: 2D models can see a whole slice at once and train on many more examples. nnU-Net (chapter 11) tries both and picks whichever does better on the data.

    Chapter 10

    Our own 3D U-Net

    We wrote a 3D U-Net from scratch, so every part is visible and we can change one thing at a time. It has 4 levels, 32 → 256 feature maps, and 5.6 million knobs. Follow one block of a lung CT through it, layer by layer. Click any step to read why it is there.

      Why these choices

      ChoiceWhy, in plain words
      Blocks of 48 × 160 × 160A whole scan does not fit in the graphics card's memory with a 3D network, so it trains on blocks. This size holds a whole tumour plus its surroundings.

      Chapter 11

      nnU-Net: a U-Net that sets itself up

      Every choice in the table above took us thought and testing. nnU-Net (Isensee and colleagues, 2021) asks: what if the computer made those choices itself? It is not a new shape. It is the same U-Net, plus a rulebook. It measures the dataset (scan size, voxel spacing, brightness), then applies fixed rules to set the block size, the halvings, the brightness scaling and more. Its authors tested it on 23 public datasets, and out of the box it matched or beat methods built specially for most of them.

        What it measures (the “fingerprint”): every scan's size and voxel spacing, and the brightness of the voxels inside the outlines. Fixed choices: InstanceNorm, LeakyReLU, Dice + cross-entropy, SGD with momentum 0.99, 1,000 rounds of training. Rule-based choices: target spacing (the median), block size (as big as the memory allows, shaped like the median scan), how many halvings and along which axes (an axis is halved only once its spacing is within 2 × of the finest one), batch size, and brightness scaling (CT: clip to the 0.5–99.5% range inside outlines, then scale; MRI: scale each scan on its own). Chosen by testing: it trains 2D, 3D and sometimes a low-resolution 3D version, with 5-fold cross-validation, and keeps whichever (or whichever mix) scores best.

        The name means “no new U-Net”: its authors' point was that careful set-up mattered more than new shapes.

        Chapter 12

        Swin UNETR: a transformer that sees further

        A filter only looks at its near neighbours. For a cell to “know” about something on the far side of the scan, the news has to pass through many layers, one neighbour at a time. Transformers, the idea behind chatbots, work differently: every piece of the input can look directly at every other piece and decide how much each one matters to it. This is called attention.

        ProblemFilters see only a small neighbourhood. A tumour's meaning can depend on things far away: the other lung, a lymph node, the chest wall.
        FixCut the scan into small patches and let each patch attend to the others. Swin UNETR puts such a transformer in the U-Net's zoom-out half and keeps the filter-based zoom-in half with skip connections.

        Click any patch to choose it. Orange = how much the chosen patch looks at each other patch.

        Why “Swin”. Letting every patch look at every other patch gets very expensive in 3D: double the patches and the work goes up four times. The Swin transformer (short for shifted windows) only lets patches attend within small windows, then, in the next layer, shifts the windows by half, so news crosses the old borders. A few layers later, everything has had a chance to reach everything else, at a fraction of the cost.

        Our results (fold 0, checking patients)

        Dice, 0 to 1, on the fold-0 checking patients (chapter 16). A single fold-0 run moves by about ±0.05 by chance alone (chapter 17), so Swin's lead is real but not yet measured precisely.

        Swin UNETR is one of the few changes that clearly helped, about 0.07 better than our U-Net on the same patients. But it is much heavier: around 62 million knobs, eleven times ours, and it took 11½ hours to train against our 5 hours. And nnU-Net, a plain U-Net set up carefully, is still ahead.

        Instead of passing notes desk to desk, the detectives hold short meetings in small groups, then reshuffle the groups and meet again, so every clue reaches every desk within a few rounds.

        Attention, in one line. Each patch makes three short lists of numbers: a query (“what am I looking for?”), a key (“what do I contain?”) and a value (“what do I pass on?”). A patch's new value is a mix of all values, weighted by how well its query matches each key. The demo uses brightness similarity as a stand-in for that match.

        The model. Swin UNETR (Hatamizadeh and colleagues, 2022) cuts the block into 2 × 2 × 2 patches, runs four Swin stages that halve the size each time, and feeds each stage's output across to a filter-based decoder, exactly like U-Net skip connections. We use MONAI's version with feature_size=48; block sizes must divide by 32.

        The catch. Transformers know less to begin with than filters do (filters assume nearby pixels matter most), so they usually need more data or pre-training. That is one reason nnU-Net's plain U-Net is hard to beat on a dataset of a few hundred scans.

        Chapter 13

        Next in line: MedSAM and others

        Every model so far finds the tumour by itself. MedSAM (Ma and colleagues, 2024) works differently: a person draws a rough box around something, and it draws the exact outline of whatever is inside. It learned from more than 1.5 million medical image–outline pairs, so it has seen a huge variety of shapes, but it does not decide what to outline. You do.

        Drag a box around something on the picture.

        Green dashed = the true outline of the lumpy shape. Orange = the outline drawn inside your box. This is a simple stand-in that shows how MedSAM is used, not the real model.

        It is shaped differently again: a very large picture reader (a transformer, run once per slice), a small part that reads the box, and a small part that combines the two into an outline. It works on flat slices, so a 3D tumour is outlined slice by slice. In our project it is the “doctor helps the AI” setting: we will give it a box around each tumour and measure how good the outline is.

        A specialist who does not go looking for suspects, but who, shown a rough area on the map, can draw the exact boundary of whatever is there faster than anyone.

        Part 4 · Preparing, training, testing fairly, improving
        Chapter 14

        Preparing the data

        Every model so far needs examples with known answers. Ours come from NSCLC-Radiomics, a public collection of lung cancer CT scans on The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA). For each of 422 patients there is a scan and an expert's outline of the main tumour. We kept the 415 with a usable outline of the main tumour and no problems in the scan. Each scan is about 112 slices of 512 × 512 pixels, one slice every 3 mm, so a single scan holds about 29 million voxels (3D pixels).

        Before a model can learn from them, three problems have to be fixed.

        1 · Brightness

        ProblemCT brightness is measured in Hounsfield units (HU): air is −1000, water 0, bone up to 3000. Lung, tumour and heart all sit in a narrow part of that range, so the full range squeezes them into a few shades of grey.
        FixKeep only −1000 to 500, the range from air to dense soft tissue, and stretch it to the full range of grey. Anything brighter, like bone, just becomes white.

        A drawn CT slice through the chest (not a real patient): body, two dark lungs, heart, spine, ribs, and a tumour in the right lung (left side of the picture, as doctors view it).

        2 · Size

        ProblemA whole scan, with all the in-between results a 3D U-Net makes from it, does not fit in a graphics card's memory.
        FixTrain on blocks cut out of the scan: 48 slices deep and 160 × 160 pixels across, about 14 × 16 × 16 cm. The model never needs the whole scan at once, because filters only look nearby (chapter 5).

        3 · Balance

        ProblemThe tumour is tiny next to the rest of the scan, about 1 voxel in 1,000. Blocks cut at random mostly show healthy lung, so the model could learn that “no tumour” is nearly always right.
        FixCentre one block in three on a randomly chosen tumour voxel. The other two thirds are still random, so the model also learns what healthy chest looks like.

        Each square is one training block seen from above. Orange = it holds some tumour; white = it does not.

        Choose how many blocks are centred on the tumour, then cut some blocks.

        The demo is a single slice, so random blocks hit the tumour more often here than in a real scan. In 3D, the block also has to land on the right slices, so random blocks miss even more often.

        The trainees don't practise on the whole city archive. Each one gets a short stack of files, trimmed to the pages that matter, and one stack in three is sure to contain a real culprit.

        From hospital files to model files. Each scan arrives as about 110 DICOM files (one per slice) plus an outline file listing points around the tumour on each slice. We join the slices into one 3D file (NIfTI, .nii.gz), keeping the pixel spacing and position, and paint the outline onto the same grid as 0s and 1s. We use only the main tumour (“GTV-1”). Lymph nodes are a separate question for later.

        The brightness rule. Clip to −1000 … 500, then map that range onto −1 … 1:

        x = (clip(hu, -1000, 500) - (-250)) / 750

        Small random changes. Each training block gets its brightness and contrast nudged by up to 10% and a little noise added, so the model doesn't rely on one scanner's exact grey levels. We don't flip left and right: the left lung has two lobes and the right has three, so the sides are not mirror images.

        Cutting a block, as in our training code:

        def sample(img, lab, size, fg_prob, rng):

        Chapter 15

        Training on lung CT

        Training works like chapter 4: guess, measure how wrong, find who caused it, nudge. It's the same loop, just on a bigger scale. Each step, the model outlines two blocks, and the loss compares its outline with the expert's. But the obvious way to measure “how wrong” fails here.

        ProblemScore the model by the share of voxels it gets right, and a model that never draws any tumour scores 97% on a block, and over 99.9% on a whole scan. That score rewards doing nothing.
        FixScore the overlap instead: the Dice score. Drawing nothing scores 0, and a perfect outline scores 1. Our loss adds it to cross-entropy, a per-voxel score from chapter 4.

        Green dashed = the expert's outline. Orange = the model's outline. Dark orange = where they overlap.

        Dice = 2 × the overlap ÷ (the expert's area + the model's area). Shared area counts twice and every extra or missing voxel counts against it, so the only way to reach 1 is to match the outline exactly. The model is trained to push Dice up, so its loss uses 1 − Dice. Cross-entropy is added because it gives every voxel its own clear signal, which makes the early steps of training steadier.

        One step, 50,000 times

        Each step is the same four moves. At first the guess is a random smudge; tens of thousands of nudges later it follows the tumour. (Drawn to show the idea: these are not saved outputs from our model.)

Covering the whole map, or none of it, earns nothing; only the right boundary scores. Every 500 rounds the chief tests them on cases they have never seen and keeps the best version of the team.

        Soft Dice. During training the model's output is a likelihood from 0 to 1 per voxel, not a hard yes or no, so Dice is computed with those likelihoods (the “overlap” is the sum of likelihood × answer). That keeps it smooth, so backpropagation can find a slope.

        def dice_ce_loss(logits, target):
    p     = softmax(logits)[:, 1]                     # likelihood of tumour, per voxel
    dice  = (2 * (p * target).sum() + 1) / (p.sum() + target.sum() + 1)
    return cross_entropy(logits, target) + (1 - dice)

        The nudge size schedule (“polynomial decay”): lr = 0.0003 × (1 − step / 50,000)0.9. Big steps early to get close, tiny steps late to settle.

        Keeping the best. The loss on training blocks keeps falling, but that only shows how well the model fits blocks it has seen. What counts is Dice on patients it has never seen (chapter 16), so those are the knobs we keep.

        Chapter 16

        Testing fairly

        A model that scores well on scans it trained on has shown only that it remembers them. The real question is how it does on patients it has never seen. So before any training, the 415 patients are split, and the split never changes. Every model in this story uses exactly the same one.

        ProblemNeighbouring slices of one scan look almost the same. Split by slice, and the model trains on slice 40 and is “tested” on slice 41 of the same person: the score looks great and means nothing.
        FixSplit by patient. Every slice of a person goes to the same group, so a test patient is someone the model has truly never met.

        One dot = one patient; bigger dot = bigger tumour. Green = trains the model, orange = checks it, dark = locked away. (Dot sizes show the idea; they are not each patient's real tumour size.)

        1. Sort the patients from smallest tumour to largest.
        2. Lock away the test group. Take them in groups of 7 and pick one from each at random: 60 patients, with small and large tumours alike. Nobody looks at how a model does on them until the very end.
        3. Deal the other 355 into 5 folds of 71, round-robin, so every fold also gets small and large tumours.
        4. Five rounds. Each round, train on 4 folds (284 patients) and check on the 5th (71). Each patient is a checking patient exactly once. These checking scores are what we use to compare ideas and pick settings.
        5. Once, at the end: the five models outline the 60 locked patients, and their answers are averaged.
        ProblemTry 20 ideas and keep whichever scores best on the test patients, and you have quietly tuned the model to those 60 people. The score no longer predicts how it does on the 61st.
        FixEvery choice (settings, cut-offs, which idea wins) is made on the checking folds. The test group is opened only for a decision that has already been made.

        A check runs after every split: no patient in both training and checking, no test patient anywhere in training, and nobody lost. The split is saved in nnU-Net's own file format, so nnU-Net, our U-Net and Swin UNETR all train and check on the very same people.

        Why average five models? Each fold's model saw a slightly different 284 patients. Averaging their likelihoods on the test patients is steadier than trusting any one of them, and it means no model is chosen by looking at test scores.

        Chapter 17

        Improving: what worked, and what didn't

        The model doesn't answer yes or no per voxel. It gives a likelihood from 0 to 1, and a cut-off turns that into an outline: above it is tumour, below it is not. The usual cut-off is 0.5. But our model was timid: near the tumour's edges it often said 0.35 or 0.4, so a 0.5 cut-off drew outlines that were too small.

        ProblemOutlines 39% too small on the test, and about 40% too small on the checking folds. Training a new model would take days.
        FixKeep the trained model and lower the cut-off. Try 0.2, 0.3, 0.4 and 0.5 on the checking folds only, and keep the best: 0.3.

        The hall of attempts

        Lots of other ideas were tried, each changing one thing from our U-Net and trained on fold 0. Its checking score to beat was 0.450.

        That top row is the most useful result of all. Retrain the same model with a different random start and the score moves by about 0.05. So almost every change lands inside the band: it may help a little or hurt a little, but one run can't tell. Only three stand clearly above it: the bigger block, the combination (which turns out to be the bigger block's gain) and Swin UNETR. One is clearly below: nnU-Net's optimizer, whose settings suit nnU-Net's own training but not ours.

        Two more clues point the same way. Using only a quarter of the patients scored about the same as using all of them, and the model scores about as well on its own training patients (0.55) as on new ones (0.54). It isn't memorising, it's under-fitting: it can't even fit what it has seen. More data won't fix that. More context will, which is exactly what a bigger block gives.

        The chief tried a dozen new routines on the team. Most made no difference you could tell apart from a good or bad week. What really helped was handing each trainee a bigger piece of the map.

        The bigger block is 64 × 192 × 192 voxels instead of 48 × 160 × 160: about twice the volume, roughly 19 × 19 × 19 cm. The model sees more of the chest around each tumour, which helps it tell a tumour from a blood vessel or the chest wall.

        Running now: the bigger block on all 5 folds with the cut-off chosen on checking, then the final test again. Every new idea now runs with two random starts, so its result can be told apart from chance. Also queued: an even bigger block, twice as many steps, half the blocks centred on tumour, and a loss that punishes missed tumour more than extra outline (Tversky).

        The hard cases. 11 of the 60 test patients are missed by both models (Dice 0.1 or less). They set a ceiling for everyone, so the next step is to look at those scans before blaming any model.

        A lung CT through the system

        Everything so far comes together here. Training is finished, and five trained models sit on disk, one per fold. A new scan arrives, one no model has seen. This is what happens to it, from file to outline.

        The result, in 3D

        A tumour is a 3D shape, and so are both outlines. Below, the scan's slices are stacked as they are in the body. Drag to turn the stack, and move the slider to step through it slice by slice, the way a doctor scrolls through a scan.

        Loading the 3D view…

        Green = the expert's outline, orange = the model's. A drawn case, not a real patient: 24 slices of the chest from chapter 14, with the tumour growing and shrinking through them.

        Notice where the two differ: mostly at the top and bottom of the tumour, where it is smallest on the slice. Those are the same thin edges where small tumours get missed (chapter 17). Judging an outline on one slice is not enough; Dice is counted over the whole 3D shape.

        The case comes in. Each of the five study groups works through the whole archive, map piece by map piece. The chief lays all their sketches over each other, keeps the boundary most of them agree on, and hands it to the senior doctor to check.

        Overlapping blocks. Blocks move half a block at a time, so every voxel is seen in several blocks and in different positions within them. A filter near a block's edge sees less around it, so averaging the overlaps evens out the weak edges. For a scan of 112 × 512 × 512 that is 4 × 6 × 6 = 144 blocks per model, 720 runs in all.

        Why keep only the largest piece? The question is “outline the main tumour” (GTV-1, chapter 14), so a small separate spot is most likely a false alarm. The catch: if a false alarm is bigger than a small real tumour, the real one gets thrown away. Keeping the piece the model is most sure of, instead of the biggest, is one of the ideas being tested.

        The output is saved in the same 3D format as the input (NIfTI), with the same spacing and position, so it can be laid over the scan in any viewer and scored against the expert's outline voxel by voxel.

        Chapter 19

        Where it goes next

        A number like “Dice 0.533” means different things to different people. Here is one result, explained three ways.

        From a research result to something a hospital could use

        Our U-Net now reaches 90% of nnU-Net's score, but neither model is ready to help with a real patient. This is the road from here, and where we are on it.

        1. A fair test on public scans. 415 patients, a locked test group, every choice made on checking folds. nnU-Net 0.591, our U-Net 0.533.
        2. Lock in the bigger block. It helped most on fold 0 (+0.095). Now running on all 5 folds, then the final test again. New ideas run twice, so chance can be told apart.
        3. Small tumours. The whole remaining gap. Ideas queued: half the blocks on tumour, a loss that punishes misses more, keeping the most confident piece. And a careful look at the 11 test patients both models miss.
        4. Another hospital's scans. Our scans all come from one hospital. Testing once on a different public collection (NSCLC-Radiogenomics, from Stanford) shows whether the model still works on other scanners and patients.
        5. How much do doctors disagree? A public set where five cancer doctors outlined the same tumours gives the real ceiling: a model can't be judged against one expert more strictly than experts agree with each other.
        6. Does it matter for treatment? In radiotherapy the outline decides where the radiation goes. The real test of an outline is whether its errors change the dose the tumour gets.
        7. Approval before any hospital data. Only public data is used now. Using a hospital's own scans needs both the hospital and the university to agree first, and the data never leaves where it is allowed to be.
        8. A medical device, with a doctor in charge. To be used on patients, software like this must be approved as a medical device. Even then it only suggests an outline; a doctor always checks and corrects it.

        The team has passed its exams on old cases. Before it works a real case, it must be tested in another city, compared with how much senior detectives disagree among themselves, and given a licence. Even then, a senior detective signs off every report.
